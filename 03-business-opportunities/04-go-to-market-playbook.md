# Go-To-Market Playbook — From Idea to First Revenue

## The Core Principle: Charge Before You Build

The biggest mistake AI founders make is building for 3 months, then trying to sell.

The right sequence:
1. **Identify the problem** (1 day of research)
2. **Find 10 people who have the problem** (1 week of conversations)
3. **Get a verbal commitment to pay** before writing code (1 week)
4. **Build the MVP** (2-4 weeks)
5. **Collect payment** (first day of launch)

You should have paying customers within 6 weeks of starting, or you're building the wrong thing.

---

## Phase 1: Problem Validation (Week 1)

### The Conversation You Need to Have

Find 10 people in your target market. Ask:

1. "Walk me through a typical week. What tasks do you do that feel like they shouldn't require your level of expertise?"
2. "If you could wave a magic wand and have one thing automated, what would it be?"
3. "What does it currently cost you (time or money) to do [the process you've identified]?"
4. "What have you tried? Why didn't it work?"
5. "If we could automate X for you for $Y/month, would that be worth it?"

Listen for pain intensity, not just interest. "That sounds cool" = not a buyer. "Oh god, that would save my life" = buyer.

### What You're Looking For

- **Frequency**: Is this a daily/weekly pain, or a rare edge case?
- **Cost**: Is the manual version expensive (in time or money)?
- **Urgency**: Have they tried to solve this before?
- **Budget authority**: Can they pay without approval? (Or who approves it?)

### Red Flags

- "We have IT/legal/compliance review everything" — 6-month sales cycle, hard for early stage
- "We'd have to run this by [3+ stakeholders]" — enterprise sales you're not ready for
- "We'd love this but we're in a budget freeze" — not a buyer now

## Phase 2: Positioning (Week 1-2)

### The Positioning Formula

```
[Product name] helps [specific job title] at [specific company type]
who struggle with [specific painful problem]
by [specific mechanism that creates the outcome]
so they can [specific valuable outcome].

Unlike [incumbent alternative], we [key differentiator].
```

Example:
> "ContractAI helps general counsel at Series A-C startups who spend 4+ hours reviewing every vendor contract by automatically extracting key clauses, flagging unusual terms, and comparing to your standard positions — so you can review a contract in 20 minutes instead of 4 hours. Unlike Kira (enterprise-only, $50K+/year), we're built for 1-10 person legal teams at $299/month."

### Pricing Strategy

**Rule of thumb:** Your price should be 10% of the value you create.

If you save a lawyer 3 hours/week × $300/hr × 50 weeks = $45,000/year in value → charge $4,500/year ($375/month).

Common mistake: underpricing out of fear. Underpricing signals low quality and attracts the wrong customers.

Start higher than you think. You can always offer discounts to early customers. You cannot raise prices on existing customers easily.

## Phase 3: Landing Page (Day 1 of Week 2)

Build a one-page site in a day (use Framer, Webflow, or even Notion):

- **Headline**: The outcome you deliver (not what the product does)
  - Bad: "AI-powered contract analysis tool"
  - Good: "Review any contract in 20 minutes"
- **3 bullet proof points**: The 3 most compelling things about your product
- **Social proof**: Even one quote from a beta user is powerful
- **Price**: Show it. Hiding price = annoying.
- **CTA**: "Start free trial" or "Book a demo" (pick one)

No demo video. No features list. No lengthy explanation. If the headline doesn't make them want to sign up, a longer page won't either.

## Phase 4: Distribution (Week 2-4)

### The Three Channels That Work for AI Products Early

**1. Direct outreach (fastest to revenue)**
- Find your ICP on LinkedIn, Twitter/X, or in niche communities
- 100 targeted DMs per week
- Personalized: "I saw you post about [their problem]. I built something that might help."
- Conversion: 2-5% → 2-5 conversations per week → 1-2 demos/week

**2. Niche communities (most efficient)**
- Every profession has a Slack, Discord, or forum
- Lawyers: r/law, lawyer Slack groups, bar association listservs
- Founders: YC community, Indie Hackers, On Deck alumni
- Doctors: physician Facebook groups, specialty medical associations
- Participate first, then mention your product once you've helped people

**3. Content marketing (slowest but compounding)**
- Write about the problem, not the product
- "How to review a contract without a lawyer" → attracts people with the problem
- SEO compounds: posts written now generate traffic in 6 months

What doesn't work early: paid ads (need product-market fit first to know messaging), cold email at scale (spam filters), press coverage (journalists don't cover pre-traction products).

## Phase 5: First 10 Customers

Your first 10 customers should feel like a manual service. That is fine.

You are learning:
- What features they actually use
- What confused them
- What they wish it did
- What they would pay more for

Treat every early customer as a research partner, not just revenue.

White-glove everything:
- Jump on calls to onboard them personally
- Respond to every support message within 1 hour
- Check in weekly: "Is this saving you time? What's not working?"
- Ask for referrals explicitly after they're happy (not before)

---

## Phase 6: Metrics That Actually Matter

At early stage (1-10 customers):
- **Activation rate**: Of signups, % that complete key action (upload first document, etc.)
- **Retention**: Are customers still paying after month 2? Month 3?
- **NPS / qualitative**: Do customers love it or just use it?
- **Time-to-value**: How quickly does a new user get their first "wow" moment?

Do NOT obsess over:
- Traffic (vanity metric until you know what traffic converts)
- Free signups (only paying customers validate the business)
- Social media followers

---

## The Decision Point at 10 Customers

After 10 paying customers, you have data to answer:

**Question 1: Are customers getting value?**
- Retention > 80% after 3 months = yes
- Retention < 60% = fundamental product problem

**Question 2: Can you acquire more like them?**
- If yes → double down on the channel that got you these 10
- If no → find the channel where your ICP hangs out

**Question 3: Is the unit economics right?**
- Revenue per customer > (AI cost + hosting + support time) × 3 = viable
- Less → either price up or reduce cost to serve

At 10 customers with strong retention, you have proof. Now you can build faster and spend on acquisition.

---

## The Speed Principle

The market for Claude-powered products is moving fast. Features that require 6 months of development to beat will be commoditized. The defensible businesses are:
1. **Network effects** — data gets better with more users (memory, learning)
2. **Deep integration** — embedded in workflows that are painful to change
3. **Domain expertise** — genuine understanding of a profession that generalists can't fake
4. **Distribution** — relationships and channels that take time to build

Build fast, get customers, build in the defensibility over time. The worst outcome is spending 6 months building something nobody wants.

**Target: first paying customer within 30 days of starting.**
